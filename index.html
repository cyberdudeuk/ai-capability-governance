Four non-negotiable principles

One route from idea to lasting public value

  • Humans remain accountable. AI never grants authority a person does not already hold.
  • AI outputs are verified, not trusted by default. Every claim traces to a citable source.
  • Public value, equality and accessibility are built in — screened before delivery, not audited after.
  • Evidence, metadata and provenance are mandatory for anything material or public-facing.

Responsible AI · start here

What do you want to do?

Start from your task. This part of the playbook routes you to the proportionate tool, template, assessment or support. Governance follows what the AI can see, create and do, not which company made it.

Quick route selector · five questions

Can I use AI for this?

1. Would the output decide, or directly change, someone’s rights, entitlements, safety or access to a service?
2. Will you use personal data or information marked OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE or above?
3. Can the AI take actions, run on a schedule or work through several steps without you?
4. Is there an approved tool in your organisation's catalogue that already does this?
5. Could a conventional rule, form or automation do the job reliably?

Your route

Answer the five questions

The route appears when every question has an answer. “Not sure” is a valid answer.

A first sort, not an approval. Any route other than an approved tool leads to a registered use case.

Six things to know first

AI concepts and limitations

Generative AI predicts plausible output. It can be fluent and wrong at once, so outputs need checking in proportion to what depends on them.

Models and providers

A product can use several models from several providers, sometimes chosen automatically. The model can change without the product name changing.

Enterprise grounding

Grounding feeds organisational content to the model. Whatever the index can reach, the AI can surface, including titles, filenames and metadata.

Agents, tools and actions

An agent plans steps and uses tools to act: send, update, create or call other agents. Acting changes the risk boundary.

Human oversight

Oversight works only if the reviewer has the competence, the evidence and the authority to challenge, override or stop.

Deployment route

The same model reached directly, through a cloud platform or inside another supplier’s product is a different governance object each time.

The playbook

Ten principles, one life cycle

The AI Playbook for the UK Government (opens an external website) (GDS, 10 February 2025) supersedes the Generative AI Framework and covers all AI: machine learning, NLP, computer vision, generative and agentic systems. The ten principles are paraphrased below; open each for its first moves.

The deployment life cycle

Playbook principle 5: manage the whole life cycle, including the unglamorous parts. Each stage has a gate; nothing moves right without passing it.

Stage 1

Discover
  • Frame the problem and the user need: is AI the right tool at all?
  • Triage risk: personal data, decisions about people, public-facing?

Assemble the multidisciplinary team; screen for DPIA and equality impacts

Gate · A named SRO and a written problem statement

Stage 2

Design
  • Decide buy vs build; source and assess the data
  • Threat-model with security; place the human-oversight points

Legal, ethics and commercial shape the approach, not the finished thing

Gate · DPIA drafted; risks owned; oversight designed in

Stage 3

Build & evaluate
  • Develop securely; verify the model and data supply chain
  • Evaluate accuracy, bias and robustness against agreed baselines
  • Red-team it: prompt injection, misuse, edge cases

Gate · Evaluation results reviewed by the whole team

Stage 4

Deploy
  • Pass assurance gates; publish the ATRS record
  • Switch on human review and incident response
  • Tell users they are interacting with AI

Gate · Assurance sign-off; transparency record live

Stage 5

Operate & retire
  • Monitor drift, bias and hallucination in production
  • Re-evaluate on every model or data update
  • Plan decommissioning and knowledge handover

Gate · Monitoring dashboards live; exit plan on file

The domains

Five lenses on every deployment

Cyber, data, digital, technology, and the legal-ethical frame around them. Each domain has its own authoritative guidance; a deployment is ready only when all five say so. That is why the team is multidisciplinary.

Cyber

Cyber security

NCSC secure AI guidelines
NCSC machine learning principles
Secure by Design (UK Government)

NCSC Guidelines for secure AI system development cover secure design, development, deployment and operation. Agreed with CISA and 21 international agencies; aimed at any system that uses AI, built or bought.

Secure by Design and the Government Cyber Security Standard apply to AI services like any other, plus AI-specific threat modelling: prompt injection, data poisoning, model extraction, over-permissioned agents.

NCSC's principles for the security of machine learning translate the threats into controls. Walk before you run with agentic AI.

Data

Data & information

Data Ethics Framework
ICO: AI and data protection
ATRS hub

The Data Ethics Framework guides appropriate, responsible data use across the public sector: transparency, accountability, fairness.

UK GDPR and the DPA 2018 require a DPIA before high-risk processing. The ICO's AI guidance, with a statutory AI code of practice in development, is the regulator's view of what good looks like. Cross-border processing (for example a US-processed model behind a UK product) needs a Transfer Risk Assessment as well as SCCs.

The Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard is mandatory for central departments and arms-length bodies: a published record for every in-scope algorithmic tool.

Model quality is data quality: govern provenance, representativeness and retention for training and grounding data alike — retrieval systems inherit the permissions, quality and currency of the estate.

Digital

Digital & service

Service Standard
AI Playbook for the UK Government
AI Knowledge Hub

The Service Standard and Service Manual set the operating model: start with user needs, work in multidisciplinary teams, iterate in the open. AI services are still services.

The AI Playbook and the AI Knowledge Hub supply the patterns, case studies and communities; the PM's AI Exemplars programme is the test-and-learn engine for scaling what works.

Accessibility is law, not polish: WCAG 2.2 AA under the Public Sector Bodies Accessibility Regulations 2018. AI interfaces (chat, voice, summaries) need their own accessibility evidence.

Technology

Technology & architecture

Technology Code of Practice
AI Opportunities Action Plan
Action Plan delivery progress

The Technology Code of Practice governs the technology choices: cloud first, interoperability, open standards, sustainability, exit.

Compute and infrastructure are national policy: AI Growth Zones and a compute roadmap from 21 ExaFLOPs (2025) towards 420 by 2030 under the AI Opportunities Action Plan.

Buy well: use Crown Commercial Service AI routes, insist on evaluation access and model-change notice in contracts, and design the exit before signing the entry.

Legal & ethics

Legal, ethics & society

Explaining decisions made with AI
Data Ethics Framework
Equality Act 2010 guidance

The Equality Act 2010 and the Public Sector Equality Duty apply to automated and AI-assisted decisions. Public-law duties (fairness, rationality, giving reasons) still bind when a model is in the loop.

Explainability has a manual: 'Explaining decisions made with AI', by the ICO and The Alan Turing Institute — and explanations shown to people should reference functional needs, never a diagnosis or label.

Public trust is earned in the open: engage affected people early, say clearly where AI is used, and give a route to challenge and redress.

Design and build · capability classes

Generative, agentic and declarative

Three ways of using the same underlying models, with different failure modes and therefore different controls. Most real deployments combine all three, which is exactly why the class needs naming before the controls are chosen.

Generative

Produces content, analysis or options on request. Nothing changes until a person acts on the output.

You specify: A prompt, grounding sources and an output format.

Drafting, summarising, translating Analysis and option generation Search and question answering Code and test generation

Primary failure Plausible but wrong output accepted without review. Tier tendency Usually low to moderate: consequence sits with the person who acts on the output.

Controls that carry the weight

  • Accuracy and grounding evaluation
  • Citation and provenance
  • Disclosure that AI was used
  • Human review before use

Agentic

Takes actions across tools and systems under delegated authority, deciding its own path to a goal.

You specify: A goal, tools, permissions, guardrails and approval thresholds.

Triage and routing Multi-step workflow execution System updates within limits Orchestration across services

Primary failure Correct-looking action taken outside intended scope, or an irreversible action taken without approval. Tier tendency Moderate to high: the agent itself changes the world, so authority and reversibility dominate.

Controls that carry the weight

  • Capability separated from authority
  • Least-privilege permissions and recertification
  • Approval gates for consequential actions
  • Full action logging and rollback

Declarative

You state the outcome, rules and boundaries; the platform resolves how they are met and keeps behaviour inside the declaration.

You specify: Instructions, knowledge sources, permitted actions and constraints — as configuration, not code.

Configured assistants defined by a manifest Policy and rules expressed as configuration Low-code flows with declared triggers Templated agents published to a team

Primary failure The declaration drifts from intent, or nobody owns the specification that is now running in production. Tier tendency Varies with what is declared: the specification is the control surface, so review it as you would code.

Controls that carry the weight

  • Version control over the declaration
  • Named owner for the specification
  • Change assessment on every edit
  • Regression tests against the declared outcome

Nine controls for every connector, plugin and MCP server

  1. Register every connector, plugin and MCP server in the tool and data-source registers, with a named owner.
  2. Record read and write scope separately. Any write tool makes the use case at least Act class.
  3. Prefer official, supplier-maintained servers over community ones, and record which is in use.

  8. Check where each connected system stores and processes data, and whether it sits inside your data boundary.

Principles with tests, gates with teeth

A three-tier canon governs everything that influences how something is built — prototypes explicitly included, because most principle breaches enter the estate through prototypes that were never intended to survive, and then did.

Tier 1 — mandated, cited not restated

T1-01 Secure by Design T1-02 CAF & GovAssure T1-03 Technology Code of Practice T1-04 GDS Service Standard T1-05 AI Playbook T1-06 Data protection & public records T1-07 WCAG 2.2 AA

Tier 1 conformance is binary: the principle applies and is met, or a recorded exception exists. No informal partial conformance.

Tier 2 — the core seven, each with a test

AP-01
Federated, not centralised

Test: Can each element be traced to a named authoritative owner outside the central platform?Looks compliant, is not: "We will federate later, once everything is in one place."

AP-02
Discovery separated from access

Test: Can a user discover an asset exists without thereby gaining access to it?Looks compliant, is not: Endpoint or credential details published as discovery metadata for convenience.

AP-03
Human-in-the-loop by design

Test: Can any automated component write to the record without recorded human approval?Looks compliant, is not: "The user can always review it" — optional review presented as a control.

AP-04
Traceability & provenance mandatory

Test: Take any output at random: can you reconstruct inputs, owner, decision and evidence without asking a person?Looks compliant, is not: Evidence linked at closure rather than at capture.

AP-05
Reuse before build

Test: Is there a recorded reuse assessment that predates the build decision?Looks compliant, is not: Reuse assessment performed after the build decision, to justify it.

AP-06
Accessible & inclusive by design

Test: Is accessibility evidence produced before release? Does every status carry a non-colour cue?Looks compliant, is not: "We will run an accessibility audit before go-live" as the only accessibility activity.

AP-07
Guardrails enforced, not documented

Test: Attempt to breach the control through the normal interface. Does it succeed?Looks compliant, is not: A mandatory gate with no exception path — guaranteeing informal workarounds.

Where should the information live?

Work down the questions in order. The first "yes" is your answer — the goal is the right information, to the right people, at the right time, securely and efficiently.

1. Is the information already in an authoritative source (for example an existing SharePoint site, policy library, DMS)?

Yes → Use existing authoritative location — keep content in place, ensure agent uses existing permissions, maintain single source of truth, lower duplication and maintenance risk.

2. Can the agent be granted access via existing permissions?

Yes → Grant access via existing permissions — respect least privilege, use groups and roles already in place, review access periodically.

3. Can permissions be adjusted for a new site (or team) without creating excessive oversharing or security risk?

Yes → Adjust permissions or create a secure location — create dedicated site or library if needed, configure granular permissions, review with the information owner.

4. Is copying or moving content necessary for the agent to function effectively?

Yes → Copy or move content with strong controls — risk of duplication and divergence, increased maintenance and update process, maintain links back to source of truth, confirm retention and records handling.

If every answer is no — reconsider the approach
  • Use metadata, views, filters or connectors
  • Use automated search over authoritative sources
  • Build a knowledge layer or index
  • Avoid unnecessary duplication

Capability is not authority

An agent may be able to do many things, but can only do what it is authorised to do. Capability is about potential; authority is about permission. Capability + authority + context = action — only when all controls are satisfied can the agent act.

Technical potential and skills: the full range of capabilities, knowledge, tools and actions the agent is built or enabled to perform.

Policies & rulesOrganisational policies, legal requirements and ethical guardrails define boundaries.

Delegated authorityExplicit permissions granted for specific tasks, data and systems.

Conditions & controlsContext, thresholds, approvals and risk checks must be satisfied.

Human oversightPeople remain in control of decisions and can intervene or override.

What the agent is allowed to do — authority

Permitted actions in the right context, within defined boundaries, for specific purposes and users.

Reduces risk Limits exposure by ensuring agents cannot act beyond intent or policy.

Improves accountability Clear ownership and delegated authority for all actions.

Supports compliance Meets legal, regulatory and ethical obligations.

Enables agility Adjust permissions without rebuilding capabilities.

Builds trust Transparent controls and oversight build confidence.

Adapts to context Decisions and permissions can vary by role, risk and situation.

DEIA by default — who this serves, and the checklist

Every product must actively serve those experiencing the greatest adversity: economic hardship, disabled and neurodivergent users, underrepresented communities, vulnerable households and carers, digital exclusion and low literacy, and everyone algorithmic unfairness would otherwise reach first.

Who is most harmed if this fails or excludes?

Does the design assume internet access or literacy?

Has it been tested with people from affected groups?

Are legal obligations (Equality Act, PSED) documented?

Is there a non-digital fallback route?

Does the data we collect reflect the full population?

Are there language and accessibility alternatives?

How will we measure equitable outcomes, not just usage?

Bias will be reproduced unless actively constrained

A model trained on historical outcomes learns the discrimination in the record, not the potential of a well-supported person. Four pre-specified bias domains to audit — and abstention ("insufficient data — human decision only") is always acceptable, and must be possible.

Allocation

Systematically fewer or lesser recommendations for protected groups with equivalent profiles.

Calibration

Predictions accurate against a biased historical record, wrong about supported potential.

Representation gap

Too few cases for a group; the model fills gaps with majority priors.

Feedback loop

Biased outputs produce worse outcomes that retrain the model — break the loop by design.

Training-data standards: exclude invalidated-treatment-era and sanctioned-employer records and proxy features; require minimum cases per group, representation audits, labelled synthetic augmentation for regularisation only, and temporal weighting toward modern practice. Lived-experience panels review real outputs; community bodies sign off the test criteria; explanations reference functional needs, never the diagnosis.

Five assurance stages, five kinds of opinion

Each stage states what it is for, what evidence it reviews, and the opinions it can legitimately reach.

Readiness

Determine whether controlled deployment can begin.

Test whether controls operate and the playbook supports decisions.

Algorithms

Barometer banding

mean of rated dimensions; 0–0.9 Nascent, 1.0–1.9 Emerging, 2.0–2.4 Establishing, 2.5–3.0 Leading; unrated dimensions excluded from the mean and reported as unrated.

Criticality to gate

sum of ten dimensions scored 1–5; 10–20 personal, 21–30 team, 31–38 departmental, 39–44 operational service, 45–50 citizen impact.

Barrier banding

impact × likelihood on 1–5 scales; 1–3 low, 4–8 medium, 9–15 high, 16–25 critical.

Unit economics

value per interaction = (minutes saved ÷ 60 × hourly rate) × quality factor; net = value − cost; return = net ÷ cost expressed as a percentage.

Validation rules

Numeric inputs Clamp to declared minimum and maximum; reject non-numeric silently rather than throwing.

Unrated dimensions Show an em dash, never a zero, and exclude from means.

Currency Two decimal places, pound sign, thousands separated; never rounded away in totals.

Clipboard Plain text only; confirm with a transient state, no alert.

Persistence One key, versioned; parse defensively and fall back to blank on any error.

Accessibility implementation

Colour Every text and meaningful non-text pair contrast-tested; accents have separate text-safe variants where a fill carries text.

Keyboard All controls are buttons or inputs; aria-pressed and aria-current reflect state; focus ring never suppressed.

Motion The guidance ticker pauses on hover and respects prefers-reduced-motion.

Assurance

Human-centred controls for trustworthy AI work

Risk-based human-in-the-loop controls make sure AI never grants authority a user does not already possess, and that every material output can be defended after the fact.

The five-stage assurance workflow

  1. Classify — identify service impact, data sensitivity, equality and accessibility risk, security risk and model/agent autonomy. Use the triage tool →
  2. Select — apply the standards, source guidance, skills, agents, conformance packs and review level the classification demands.
  3. Execute — work with least privilege, mandatory source citation, deterministic checks and pair or buddy support where competence is thin.
  4. Review — 4-eyes or 6-eyes review, specialist assurance, and no self-approval for material or high-risk outputs.
  5. Learn — capture evidence, incidents, near-misses, feedback, source drift and model changes; feed them back into the corpus.

The H0–H5 human review model

Review levels, when they apply and the evidence each produces
LevelNameWhat it meansTypical examplesEvidence
H0No review neededPersonal, throwaway, non-published exploration with no personal or sensitive data.Explaining a concept to yourself; drafting private notes.None required.
H1Self-checkAuthor verifies facts, sources and accessibility before use. Low impact, easily reversible.Internal meeting notes, first-draft ideas.AI-use note in the document.
H2Peer checkA second competent person checks output before it leaves the team.Internal reports, non-public analysis, low-risk code in a sandbox.Reviewer name, date, issues found.
H3Specialist review (4-eyes)A named specialist in the relevant discipline reviews. No self-approval.Accessibility, security, legal, statistical or clinical content; production code.Specialist sign-off + test results.
H4Multi-specialist review (6-eyes)Two or more specialisms plus the accountable owner. Independent challenge required.Public-facing decisions, casework support, anything affecting entitlements.Panel record, dissent captured, residual risk stated.
H5Governance board and publicationFormal board approval, published transparency record and monitored deployment.Automated or semi-automated decisions about people; national services.ATRS record, DPIA, EQIA, monitoring plan, review date.

Reviewer checklist

Always check

  • Every citation opens, exists and says what the text claims
  • Numbers, dates, thresholds and legal references independently verified
  • Nothing asserts compliance that has not actually been tested
  • No personal or sensitive data was entered into an unapproved tool
  • Accessibility and plain-language requirements met, not assumed
  • Assumptions, gaps and residual risk stated explicitly
  • Reviewer is independent of the author at H3 and above

Common failure patterns

  • • Confident fabrication — plausible citations that do not exist
  • • Stale authority — quoting withdrawn or superseded guidance
  • • Compliance theatre — a checklist ticked without a test run
  • • Silent scope creep — an agent doing more than was authorised
  • • Automation bias — reviewers rubber-stamping fluent output
  • • Averaged users — outputs tuned to a majority that excludes minorities
  • • Evidence gaps — no record of who checked what, or when

DEIA in AI specifically

Known equity risks of AI-assisted delivery. Performance that degrades for under-represented groups; language models that default to majority dialects and names; outputs written above the reading age of the audience; automation that removes the human route people in crisis rely on; accessibility regressions in AI-generated code and content; and reviewers who trust fluent text more than accurate text.

What we do about them. Screen every change with the DEIA tool; disaggregate evaluation by affected group where lawful and proportionate; keep a human route that cannot be removed by efficiency targets; test AI-generated interfaces with assistive technology before merge; publish limitations honestly; and give people a real way to challenge an outcome.

Code of practice and conduct

Responsible use of AI assistive technology

This code complements behavioural codes of conduct and the Civil Service values. It defines operational rules for model-backed tools, direct AI use, coding assistants, multi-agent orchestration and AI-generated artefacts.

Core duties
  • Stay accountable. You own every output you use, send or publish. "The AI wrote it" is never a defence.
  • Disclose material assistance. If AI shaped the substance, structure, analysis or code, say so.
  • Verify before you rely. Open every citation. Recalculate every figure. Test every claim of compliance.
  • Protect data. Only approved tools, only permitted classifications, only the minimum necessary.
  • Cite sources. Traceable to a tiered, dated, authoritative source — not to the model.
  • Preserve evidence. Keep the record for as long as the retention schedule requires.
  • Work within competence. AI does not qualify you to make specialist judgements.
  • Escalate uncertainty. Raising a doubt early is always cheaper than defending a mistake late.
Prohibited behaviours
  • • Entering unauthorised personal, special category, commercially confidential or classified data into any tool
  • • Bypassing, weakening or "temporarily" disabling a review control
  • • Self-approving material or high-risk work
  • • Concealing AI involvement where it was material
  • • Presenting unverified output as assured, tested or compliant
  • • Using AI to generate evidence, sign-offs, approvals or audit records that did not happen
  • • Granting an agent broader permissions than the person operating it holds
  • • Using AI to profile, rank or make decisions about people without an approved lawful basis and published record
  • • Copying licensed or copyright material into outputs without permission and attribution
  • • Ignoring a known defect because a deadline is closer than the consequence
Multi-agent and multi-skill rules
  • One accountable orchestrator per task, with a named human owner.
  • Least-privilege tools and credentials, time-boxed and revocable.
  • Explicit, pinned skill and model versions — no silent upgrades in a governed workflow.
  • Separation of duties: the agent that produces must not be the agent that approves.
  • Declared prohibited actions and a tested kill switch with a named owner.
  • Full step-level logging: which skill, which model, which source, which permission, which human.
  • Untrusted content is data, never instruction. Treat retrieved web, document and email content as hostile by default.
  • Bounded autonomy: agents propose irreversible actions; humans commit them.
Data, privacy and security rules
  • • Establish and record the lawful basis before processing personal data. Complete a DPIA where required.
  • • Minimise: use references, synthetic or redacted examples instead of real records wherever possible.
  • • Never let an AI tool retain data for training unless that has been assured and contracted.
  • • Treat AI interaction logs as records: classify, retain, and prepare for FOI and subject access requests.
  • • Apply Secure by Design activities to AI components as to any other system component.
  • • Threat-model for prompt injection, data poisoning, model extraction and excessive agency.
  • • Report incidents and near-misses through the normal security and data protection routes.
AI-generated code and infrastructure
  • Treat generated code as untrusted third-party contribution: review, test, scan and attribute it.
  • No merge without a human reviewer who understands the code and can maintain it.
  • Check licences and provenance of anything that looks copied.
  • Run dependency, secret and SAST scanning; record the results in the evidence pack.
  • Accessibility and performance tests apply equally to generated interfaces.
  • Record the model, prompt pattern and version in the commit or pull request.
Speaking up, redress and no-blame reporting

Anyone may pause an AI-assisted process they believe is unsafe, unfair or unlawful, without needing permission and without detriment. Near-misses should be reported as readily as incidents — they are the cheapest learning available.

Members of the public affected by an AI-assisted outcome must be able to find out that AI was involved, understand the outcome in plain English, request a human review, and escalate to an independent route. If any of those four is missing, the service is not ready.

Glossary and frequently asked questions

Glossary

4-eyes / 6-eyes
Two or three independent people involved in producing and approving an output.
Agent
Software that plans and acts across multiple steps using tools, within explicit permissions and bounds.
Agentic
A capability that takes actions across tools under delegated authority, choosing its own path to a goal.
Assisted digital
Support for people who cannot use a digital service independently.
Assurance tier
The proportionate level of control an agent attracts, derived from criticality rather than popularity.
ATRS
Algorithmic Transparency Recording Standard — the UK format for publishing algorithmic tool use.
Authority
What an agent is permitted to do, as distinct from what it is technically capable of doing.
Automation bias

Frequently asked questions

Do I have to declare every use of AI?

No. Declare material assistance — where AI shaped the substance, structure, analysis or code of an output. Trivial uses such as spell-checking do not need a declaration, but your local policy may set a lower threshold.

Can AI approve my work?

No. AI can suggest, draft, check and explain. Approval is a human act by someone with the authority to make that decision. AI never grants authority a user does not already hold.

What if the AI cites a source that does not exist?

Treat it as an incident. Do not publish. Verify every citation against the original, log the failure in the drift and incident register, and check whether the same prompt pattern has been used elsewhere.

Is this a substitute for a DPIA, EQIA or security assurance?

No. This toolkit helps you prepare and evidence those assessments; it does not replace them or the professionals accountable for them.

Can I paste official-sensitive information into a general assistant?

Not unless the tool is explicitly approved for that classification and the data-handling terms have been assured. When in doubt, do not paste — ask your information asset owner.

How do I handle AI-generated code?

Treat it as untrusted third-party code: review it, test it, scan dependencies, record provenance and never merge without a human reviewer who understands it.

What accessibility duties apply to AI-generated content?

The same ones that apply to everything else. WCAG 2.2 AA, plain language, alternative formats and the accessibility statement duty all apply regardless of who or what drafted the content.

Who do I contact if something goes wrong?

Your service owner and the named AI assurance contact for your organisation. Report near-misses too — they are the cheapest form of learning.

Gaps and roadmap

What is missing, prioritised with MoSCoW

An honest backlog is a trust signal. These are the gaps identified in this portal and in most public sector AI guidance, ordered by whether they are essential, valuable, desirable or deliberately out of scope.

Must have

Named accountable owner and published contact route

Nothing on this page is credible without a person who owns it and answers questions.

Must have

Accessibility statement, testing evidence and feedback route

Legally required for UK public sector sites; also the fastest trust signal.

Must have

Version, last-reviewed and next-review dates on every artefact

Freshness is the main failure mode of guidance portals.

Must have

Machine-readable metadata schema published openly

Without a schema, registries cannot federate and evidence cannot be automated.

Must have

Risk-based human review model with no self-approval

The core control that keeps accountability with people.

Must have

Source citation and provenance for every claim

Prevents hallucination becoming policy.

Must have

DEIA screening built into the default workflow

Equality duty is a due-regard duty — it must happen before decisions, not after.

Should have

Search across the whole corpus with filters and permalinks

Findability is the top usability complaint for guidance sites.

Should have

Role-based entry points and task-first navigation

Users arrive with a task, not an interest in your information architecture.

Should have

Downloadable, offline and printable formats

Supports assisted digital, low bandwidth and audit contexts.

Should have

Worked examples and anti-patterns for each control

Abstract principles do not change behaviour; examples do.

Should have

Change log and subscribe-to-updates

Lets teams re-check work when guidance changes.

Should have

Multilingual and alternative-format provision (Welsh, Easy Read, BSL)

Statutory in some contexts and essential for inclusion in all.

Should have

Automated link-health and freshness checking

Broken authority links silently destroy trust.

Could have

API and JSON export of the registry and corpus

Enables reuse by other departments and tooling.

Could have

Maturity self-assessment with benchmarking

Helps organisations see where they are without a consultant.

Could have

Community contribution workflow with moderation

Scales the corpus beyond a single team.

Could have

Interactive decision tree for tool selection

Reduces the "which tool may I use?" support burden.

Could have

Carbon and cost telemetry per model call

Makes proportionality measurable rather than rhetorical.

Won't have (this time)

Automated approval of any high-risk output

Out of scope by design — approval stays human.

Won't have (this time)

Storing personal or official-sensitive data in this portal

The portal is public and stateless; sensitive records belong in assured systems.

Won't have (this time)

Vendor-specific lock-in to a single model provider

Conflicts with interoperability and exit obligations.

Won't have (this time)

Replacing professional assurance roles

The toolkit supports specialists; it does not substitute for them.
